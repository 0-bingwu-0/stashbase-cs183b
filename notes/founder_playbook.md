# Founder Playbook

A decision manual for building a startup, distilled from Stanford CS183B (*How to Start a Startup*). Organized around the questions founders actually face — not lectures. Each entry stands on its own: read only the one you need.

**How to use this:** Find your question. Read the Short Answer for the decision. Use the Key Principles to reason, the Framework to choose, the Checklist to act, and Common Mistakes to avoid the predictable traps. Follow Related Questions to connect ideas.

**The five things that recur everywhere in this course, if you remember nothing else:**
1. Make something a small number of people *love*. Everything else is downstream of this.
2. Talk to users and do things that don't scale — personally, constantly, from day one.
3. Execution and focus beat ideas. Say no to almost everything.
4. Hiring (starting with your cofounder) is the highest-leverage decision you make. Go slow; bet the company on each early person.
5. Momentum is the lifeblood. Growth and morale feed each other; losing either spirals.

# Ideas

## How do I find a startup idea?

### Short Answer
Don't sit down and try to brainstorm startup ideas — you'll generate plausible-sounding bad ones. Instead, become the kind of person who notices ideas: learn deeply, get to the leading edge of a field, and build things you and people you respect actually want. The best ideas arrive as side projects you can't stop working on.

### Key Principles
- Turn your mind into one that *has* ideas unconsciously, rather than forcing ideas consciously.
- Live at the leading edge of some technology or industry; from there, missing pieces look obvious to you and prescient to everyone else.
- The best ideas are things you yourself need — you become your own first user.
- Cofounders and ideas often come from the same place: working on interesting problems with people you like.
- Genuine interest sustains the years of grind that an idea you merely "thought was a good business" cannot.

### Framework
- If an idea came from a real problem in your own life → strong starting point.
- If it came from "what would be a good startup?" → suspect it; it's probably plausible-but-bad.
- If you'd work on it even as a side project with no expectation of a company → pursue it.
- If you need to convince yourself it's exciting → keep looking.

### Checklist
- [ ] Identified a problem you personally experience or have deep domain knowledge of
- [ ] Spent real time immersed in the relevant industry (even working in it)
- [ ] Can state the problem in one sentence
- [ ] Confirmed others have the same problem by talking to them
- [ ] The idea has a "kernel" that can grow in interesting directions over a decade

### Common Mistakes
- **Brainstorming ideas in the abstract.** Why: it feels productive and entrepreneurial. Better: follow genuine curiosity into a domain until problems surface on their own.
- **Picking an idea because it sounds like a good business.** Why: it's defensible to others. Better: pick what you'd build anyway; passion is the fuel for the hard years.
- **Waiting for a "big" idea.** Why: ambition makes starting small feel unworthy. Better: start with a small, specific, true problem that can expand.

## What makes an idea worth pursuing?

### Short Answer
A good idea usually *sounds* bad — if it sounded obviously good, too many people would already be doing it. What matters is whether you have a non-obvious insight ("you sound crazy but you're right"), a fast-growing market, a path to expand from a tiny beachhead, and a real reason this is the moment. Ideas are cheap, but the *quality* of the idea still sets your ceiling, because great execution on a terrible idea goes nowhere.

### Key Principles
- "Idea" is broad: it includes market, growth rate, expansion path, and defensibility — not just the product concept.
- The best ideas look terrible at first (Google was the 13th search engine; Facebook the 10th social network; Airbnb sounded insane).
- You want a contrarian truth: something smart, informed people disagree with — *and* you know why they're wrong.
- Care more about the *growth rate* of a market than its current size. You're surfing a wave; pick a rising one.
- Clones and "X but for people who like red wine" companies almost always fail. Derivative ideas attract no true believers.

### Framework
Pressure-test an idea against these:
- **Why now?** Why is this the perfect moment — not two years ago, not two years from now? If there's no good answer, be skeptical.
- **Contrarian check:** Can you name what you know that the doubters don't? If you can't articulate it, you're just isolated, not contrarian.
- **Expansion path:** Can you dominate a small market and then grow concentrically (Amazon: books → everything)?
- **Market trajectory:** Is the market growing fast on its own? You cannot create a market that doesn't want to exist.

### Checklist
- [ ] The idea sounds slightly crazy to smart people
- [ ] You can articulate the specific insight others are missing
- [ ] There's a compelling "why now"
- [ ] You can name a small market you could plausibly dominate first
- [ ] The market is growing, not flat or shrinking
- [ ] It's not a clone with a made-up differentiator

### Common Mistakes
- **Chasing ideas that sound good.** Why: validation feels safe. Better: good ideas look bad and are therefore uncrowded.
- **Targeting a giant market on day one.** Why: big TAM feels ambitious. Better: own a niche so small others dismiss it, then expand.
- **Ignoring market growth because the current product works.** Why: present traction is seductive. Better: confirm the wave is rising.

## Should I work on a problem I personally have?

### Short Answer
Yes, when you can — it's one of the biggest advantages in early-stage work. Building something you need makes you your own first user, gives you taste and conviction, and keeps you going through the grind. If you *don't* have the problem yourself, you're at a real disadvantage and must compensate by getting extraordinarily close to people who do.

### Key Principles
- Mission-driven founders building for themselves out-iterate and out-last others.
- Being your own user shortens the feedback loop to nearly zero — you feel every flaw.
- If it's not your problem, embed with users: work in their office, talk to them daily, even do their job.
- The best companies are mission-oriented; people work harder on something that matters to them.
- Conviction born from personal need lets you ignore bad early signals that would scare off a dispassionate founder.

### Framework
- You have the problem → build for yourself; validate by your own daily use.
- You don't have the problem but can immerse → go become a "cog in the industry" for a month or two first (the Homejoy founders trained as cleaners).
- You don't have it and can't get close → reconsider the idea; you're flying blind.

### Checklist
- [ ] You (or your cofounder) use the product and would miss it if it disappeared
- [ ] If not a personal problem, you've spent weeks inside the users' world
- [ ] You can describe the user's day better than they can
- [ ] You talk to real users at least several times a week

### Common Mistakes
- **Building for a customer you've never been and never met.** Why: the idea seemed lucrative. Better: pick problems you live, or relocate yourself into the users' lives.
- **Assuming you understand a market from the outside.** Why: overconfidence. Better: do the actual work users do; learn why incumbents are inefficient from the inside.

## How important is market size, and how should I think about it?

### Short Answer
Think about *growth rate*, not just today's size, and about your *expansion path*, not just your beachhead. Counterintuitively, you want to start in a market so small that others think it's worthless — small enough that you can dominate it — then expand outward. Targeting a giant market on day one makes you a minnow in an ocean with countless competitors you can't even see.

### Key Principles
- A fast-growing small market beats a large stagnant one — you'd rather ride a rising wave.
- Start where you can win 60–90% share quickly (Facebook: 60% of Harvard in 10 days; PayPal: ~25–30% of eBay power sellers in months).
- Dominate one niche, then expand concentrically; don't try to boil the ocean.
- A monopoly's value lives in the future — durability matters more than today's revenue.
- Beware the "intersection of small markets" pitch ("social + mobile + sharing"); it's usually a doomed company describing nothing.

### Framework
- If the market is huge *today* and you'd have tons of competitors on day one → likely wrong altitude; find a narrower wedge.
- If the market is tiny but you can own it and it's growing → ideal beachhead.
- If you can't picture how a small win expands into something large → the kernel is too weak.

### Checklist
- [ ] Identified a small, specific market you can dominate first
- [ ] Confirmed the broader market is growing
- [ ] Mapped a concentric expansion path from the beachhead
- [ ] Avoided defining your market as an intersection of buzzwords

### Common Mistakes
- **Pitching a "$X-trillion market" on day one.** Why: it sounds ambitious to investors. Better: lead with a market small enough to own — the clean-tech companies that chased the entire energy market all died.
- **Confusing current size with opportunity.** Why: big numbers feel safe. Better: weight growth rate and your ability to capture share.

## Should I worry about competitors and being copied?

### Short Answer
Mostly no. Competitors making noise in the press crush more startup momentum than almost any external factor — but a competitor only matters once they're beating you with a real, shipped product. Assume any good idea will be copied; your defense is moving fast, making users love you, and building durable advantages, not secrecy. Aim to be the *last* mover in your category, not the first.

### Key Principles
- "The competitor to be feared is one who never bothers about you at all, but goes on making his own business better all the time."
- Don't react to competitors until they ship something that actually beats you.
- Secrecy is overrated; the cost of staying stealth (no user feedback) usually exceeds the cost of being copied.
- Last-mover advantage beats first-mover advantage — the goal is to make the *last* great improvement in a category and hold it.
- Durable moats come from proprietary tech (a 10x improvement), network effects, economies of scale, or brand — not from hiding.

### Framework
- Competitor is making press noise but hasn't beaten you → ignore; keep shipping.
- Competitor ships something genuinely better → study it, respond on product.
- Considering staying stealth to avoid copying → launch instead, unless it genuinely takes tens of millions just to start.
- Choosing a market → favor ones where you can build a lasting moat, not just be first.

### Checklist
- [ ] You're tracking your own growth, not competitors' press
- [ ] You launched rather than waited to avoid being copied
- [ ] You can name your durable advantage (tech, network, scale, or brand)
- [ ] You only respond to competitors who've shipped something better

### Common Mistakes
- **Losing momentum reacting to competitor PR.** Why: fear and ego. Better: treat press noise as irrelevant until there's a real product threat.
- **Staying stealth to prevent copying.** Why: belief that imitation is cheaper than innovation. Better: launch and out-iterate; feedback is worth more than secrecy.
- **Chasing first-mover status.** Why: it sounds like an advantage. Better: aim to be the durable last mover.

## When should I pivot?

### Short Answer
Pivot when your honest confidence in your core thesis is steadily declining and intense problem-solving hasn't reversed it — not merely because things are hard (everything is hard). If you've been fully executing and seen 3–4 weeks of no growth or backward growth, it's time to seriously consider it. Pivot *into* something you genuinely want, not randomly; most great companies are not built on pivots.

### Key Principles
- Track confidence in your thesis like a metric: is it rising or falling? That's your signal, not your mood.
- Difficulty ≠ wrong thesis. A "valley of despair" is normal even when the thesis is sound (PayPal burned ~$12M/month with near-zero revenue and still held its vision).
- Don't wait until you crash into the wall; pivot before you're out of runway.
- Successful pivots go *toward* something the founders themselves want (Airbnb's founders needed rent money and had spare space).
- Hold the long-term vision constant while changing the tactics — PayPal went Palm Pilot → email → mass-merchant, same vision throughout.

### Framework
- Confidence steady or rising, just hard → keep going, problem-solve harder.
- Confidence declining + intense problem-solving failed → pivot seriously.
- Fully executing, 3–4 weeks of flat/negative growth → reconsider direction.
- Pivot candidate is something *you* want and believe → good. A random new idea to chase money → bad.

### Checklist
- [ ] You're tracking thesis confidence, not just emotions
- [ ] You've genuinely tried intense problem-solving before concluding
- [ ] You've been fully executing (not half-trying) during the flat period
- [ ] Any pivot is toward something you actually want to build
- [ ] You're keeping the long-term vision while changing tactics

### Common Mistakes
- **Pivoting because it's hard.** Why: founders mistake difficulty for being wrong. Better: separate difficulty from declining thesis confidence.
- **Pivoting too late (crashing first).** Why: hope and sunk cost. Better: act when confidence drops, while you still have runway.
- **Pivoting randomly to whatever's hot.** Why: desperation. Better: pivot only into something you'd genuinely commit years to.

# Product

## What should I build first?

### Short Answer
Build the smallest version that genuinely solves the core problem for a few real users — the "viable" in MVP is the part people skip. Start far simpler than you think you can: take over a tiny, specific use case and do it extremely well. Do things manually before you automate, and ship even when it's a little broken; temporary brokenness beats permanent paralysis.

### Key Principles
- Start with a smaller subset of the problem than you think is the smallest possible.
- Underline *viable*: an MVP must actually solve the problem, not just be minimal.
- Do things manually first — you learn what to automate only by doing it by hand (Homejoy learned its hiring filter from manual interviews).
- Optimize for the next stage of growth (10→100 users), not for millions you don't have.
- Storyboard the entire user experience — discovery, signup, core use, follow-up — before you write code.

### Framework
- At 10 users → build for getting to 100, not to 10 million.
- Feature would take a month → find a 3–4 day version, or duplicate/duct-tape rather than block.
- Choosing between manual and automated → do it manually until you understand the pattern, then automate.
- Tempted to add an edge case → handle the generic case first; perfection now is irrelevant.

### Checklist
- [ ] Defined the single core problem your v1 solves
- [ ] Storyboarded the full user flow end to end
- [ ] Cut the scope to the smallest *viable* version
- [ ] Chose manual processes over automation where it speeds learning
- [ ] Optimized for the next order of magnitude of users, not a distant one

### Common Mistakes
- **Building a "minimal" product that doesn't actually work.** Why: rushing to ship. Better: keep it minimal but ensure it genuinely solves the problem.
- **Automating too early.** Why: engineers love systems. Better: do it by hand until you know what the system should do.
- **Building for scale you don't have.** Why: ambition and fear. Better: solve for 10→100; worry about a million when you have a hundred thousand.

## How do I know if users actually want my product (product-market fit)?

### Short Answer
The clearest signal is retention: plot what fraction of each cohort keeps using the product over time. If the curve flattens into a line parallel to the x-axis, you have a real, sticky business; if it keeps sloping to zero, you don't have fit yet. The other tells are organic word-of-mouth growth and users who'd be genuinely upset if you disappeared. You usually don't have fit when you think you do.

### Key Principles
- Retention is the truest measure of fit; a flat cohort curve means a core of users genuinely need you.
- Growth by word-of-mouth — users telling friends without prompting — is the lived sign of fit.
- "Small number love" beats "large number like": it's far easier to expand from love than to manufacture it.
- The #1 startup mistake is running growth tactics before you actually have fit; a bad product won't grow no matter how hard you push.
- Vanity metrics (total signups) lie; watch cohort retention, active usage, and whether people would be bummed if you vanished.

### Framework
- Cohort retention curve flattens above zero → likely product-market fit; now you can think about growth.
- Curve slopes to zero → no fit; go back to the product, don't hire a growth team.
- Users return without reminders → strong signal.
- Users need constant prompting to come back → weak signal.

### Checklist
- [ ] You're measuring cohort retention, not just signups
- [ ] The retention curve flattens above zero
- [ ] You're getting organic word-of-mouth growth
- [ ] Users say they'd be very disappointed if the product disappeared
- [ ] You haven't mistaken a press spike or paid traffic for real demand

### Common Mistakes
- **Declaring fit too early.** Why: optimism and pressure. Better: demand a flattening retention curve before scaling.
- **Pouring fuel (growth, ads) on a leaky bucket.** Why: growth feels like progress. Better: fix retention first; growth on a bad product just churns.
- **Trusting vanity metrics.** Why: big numbers feel good. Better: track retention, engagement, and word-of-mouth.

## How do I make a product users love, not just like?

### Short Answer
Nail functionality first, then add delight — wit on top of a product that doesn't work backfires. Treat the relationship like dating then marriage: first impressions matter disproportionately, and long-term love requires continuous energy. The startup's one sustainable edge over big competitors is customer intimacy: caring more, and showing it, in every small moment.

### Key Principles
- Two kinds of quality: "taken-for-granted" (it works) and "enchanting" (it delights). Earn the first before attempting the second.
- Design every first moment — first login, first email, first error, first support reply — as a chance to delight.
- Relationships decay without energy; show users you keep improving for them (e.g., a "since you've been gone" list of new features).
- One detractor with a terrible experience can reverse the progress of ten champions — so make bad experiences right.
- Customer intimacy is the one path to market dominance available to a startup with no money — it costs only humility and manners.

### Framework
- Product doesn't reliably work yet → fix functionality before adding personality.
- Functionality solid → invest in enchanting details and first moments.
- A user has a bad experience → over-correct and make it right; they often become your biggest champion.
- Deciding where to spend delight → target the moments every user hits (onboarding, errors, support).

### Checklist
- [ ] Core functionality is reliable before adding "delight"
- [ ] First-use moments are deliberately designed
- [ ] You have a mechanism to show ongoing improvement to existing users
- [ ] Bad experiences are actively rescued, not ignored
- [ ] Support is treated as a relationship, not a cost center

### Common Mistakes
- **Adding humor/polish before the product works.** Why: it's fun and feels like personality. Better: functionality first, enchantment second.
- **Stonewalling unhappy users.** Why: avoidance under load. Better: respond; silence is the fastest path to churn.
- **Setting expectations you can't sustain.** Why: eagerness to wow. Better: under-promise; the best way to exceed expectations is to set none.

## How simple should my product and pitch be?

### Short Answer
Radically simple. If it takes more than one sentence to explain what you do, it's almost always too complicated. Simplicity isn't only about communication — it forces you to do one thing extremely well. The most important companies in history can be described in a few words ("you can change the world in 140 characters").

### Key Principles
- A great idea fits in one clear sentence; struggling to explain it is a warning sign.
- Simplicity in the product forces focus on doing one thing exceptionally.
- Positioning is make-or-break for early users: Homejoy went from a complex pitch users tuned out to "get your place cleaned for $20 an hour," and traction followed.
- Editing — relentless simplification — is a core part of the founder's job.
- Sell one or two clear differentiators, not fifty small advantages users can't process.

### Framework
- Can't explain it in a sentence → simplify the product or the positioning until you can.
- Listing many small advantages → cut to the one or two that clearly differentiate you.
- Pitch makes users' eyes glaze → it's too complicated; find the single sharp value proposition.

### Checklist
- [ ] You can say what the product does in one sentence (passes the "mom test")
- [ ] The v1 does one thing extremely well
- [ ] Your positioning leads with one or two clear differentiators
- [ ] You've cut features and words that don't serve the core

### Common Mistakes
- **A multi-sentence explanation of what you do.** Why: founders love their full vision. Better: lead with one sentence; depth comes later.
- **Selling fifty advantages.** Why: more reasons feels more persuasive. Better: one or two clear wins users can grasp instantly.

## Should I launch now or keep building?

### Short Answer
Launch now, in almost all cases. Staying stealth to perfect a product denies you the user feedback that actually makes it good, and it won't protect you — assume anyone with a good idea will be fast-followed regardless. Unless your product genuinely needs tens of millions of dollars just to start, there's no reason to wait. You can launch a hypothesis test in an hour.

### Key Principles
- The feedback you get from launching is worth far more than the protection of secrecy.
- Imitation is not cheaper than innovation; hiding doesn't save you — shipping and out-iterating does.
- You can test the core hypothesis with an almost-fake launch (DoorDash started as a static page with PDF menus and a personal cell number).
- Launch to test whether people even want this, then build the real thing from what you learn.
- Optimize early code for speed of learning, not scalability or cleanliness.

### Framework
- Haven't validated the core hypothesis → do a minimum viable launch this week.
- Worried about being copied → launch anyway; out-iterating beats hiding.
- Product genuinely requires huge capital to exist at all → the rare case where some pre-launch building is justified.

### Checklist
- [ ] Defined the single hypothesis your launch will test
- [ ] Built the cheapest possible version to test it (even manual/fake-backend)
- [ ] Released to real users instead of polishing in private
- [ ] Set up a way to capture feedback immediately

### Common Mistakes
- **Perfecting in stealth.** Why: fear of judgment and copying. Better: launch rough; feedback compounds faster than polish.
- **Over-engineering before validation.** Why: pride in clean systems. Better: duct-tape it, learn, then rebuild.

## How do I decide which features to build?

### Short Answer
Don't build features just because users request them — get to the underlying problem behind the request, then solve that in the simplest way. Existing users ask for tweaks to optimize their current use; the highest-leverage insights come from understanding problems and watching what non-users are blocked by. Build the smallest version of an idea to test it before investing heavily.

### Key Principles
- A feature request is a clue to a deeper problem, not an order — find the real problem first.
- Existing power users optimize for the present; non-users reveal the barriers that limit your whole market.
- Solve problems, don't ship requests; users asked for faster horses, not cars.
- Build the smallest version (one to two weeks) to test what works before committing.
- Watch for anomalies in usage data — unexpected behavior often reveals the real product (PayPal found its market in eBay sellers it initially dismissed).

### Framework
- A user asks for feature X → ask why; uncover the problem; consider whether solving it differently is better.
- One customer demands a feature → wait for a pattern, or make delivery a signed contract term.
- Choosing among directions → build the cheapest test of each; let evidence decide.
- Reducing the "knowledge gap" → prefer making the product more intuitive over adding features (features widen the gap).

### Checklist
- [ ] You've traced each request back to the underlying problem
- [ ] You're weighting non-user barriers, not just power-user requests
- [ ] You build the smallest testable version before investing
- [ ] You're watching usage anomalies for hidden opportunities
- [ ] You resist adding features that increase complexity for everyone

### Common Mistakes
- **Building exactly what customers ask for.** Why: it feels responsive. Better: solve the deeper problem; literal requests often miss it.
- **Building a custom feature for one loud customer.** Why: chasing a deal. Better: wait for a pattern; nine times out of ten they find another reason not to buy.
- **Adding features to fix usability.** Why: more seems better. Better: reduce the knowledge required to use what you have.

# Users & Customer Discovery

## How do I talk to users and run user interviews?

### Short Answer
Talk about their problems and behavior, never your features — if you ask people what they want, you get "a faster horse." Have real conversations (in person or over video, never email surveys), record them so you can actually listen, and dig into anything surprising. Then validate with a prototype and a commitment test: would they actually pay or take a concrete action, not "does this sound good?"

### Key Principles
- Stay as far from features as possible; understand the problem and let yourself invent the solution.
- Who you talk to matters as much as what you ask — segment users, buyers, non-users, and competitors' users.
- After ~6–8 people in a segment you see the patterns; diversity within those people matters more than raw count.
- Record interviews instead of taking notes; playback both frees you to listen and powerfully convinces your team later.
- The money test cuts through politeness: if they won't put down $5 (or take a real commitment), they're not actually excited.

### Framework
- Want to discover problems → ask open questions about their life and current workarounds; "tell me more."
- Want to validate a solution → show a prototype and ask for a real commitment ($ or action), not approval.
- 7 say one thing, 1 says something weird → dig into the weird one; anomalies are signal.
- Existing users ask for polish; non-users name the real barriers → weight non-user feedback for big bets.

### Checklist
- [ ] Interviewing the right segments, including non-users and buyers
- [ ] Asking about problems and behavior, not feature approval
- [ ] Doing it live (in person/video), recording for playback
- [ ] Digging into surprising or contradictory answers
- [ ] Validating with a real commitment test, not "would you use this?"

### Common Mistakes
- **Asking "would you use/pay for this?"** Why: it's the obvious question. Why it fails: people say yes to be nice. Better: get a real commitment.
- **Showing the product first.** Why: you're proud of it. Why it fails: users get lost in features and can't tell you the real problem. Better: start with their life.
- **Only talking to who's easy to reach.** Why: convenience. Better: seek non-users and competitors' users — they hold the biggest insights.

## How much user research is enough?

### Short Answer
For discovery, about 6–8 interviews per distinct user segment usually surfaces the patterns — but keep talking to users *forever*, because the people who get you started in the first six months aren't the ones using you in three years. Research isn't a phase you finish; founders who stop talking to users drift away from the market. Balance it: the goal is to learn enough to act, then act.

### Key Principles
- Diminishing returns set in after ~6–8 interviews per segment; more of the same people teaches little new.
- Diversity of who you talk to beats sheer quantity.
- User contact is continuous, not a one-time discovery sprint — markets and users change.
- Surveys mostly capture the extremes (people who love or hate you); conversation reaches the middle.
- Don't over-research as procrastination; temporary brokenness and shipping teach faster than endless study.

### Framework
- Just need to understand a problem → ~6–8 diverse interviews per segment is enough to find patterns.
- Patterns are clear and consistent → stop interviewing, start building/testing.
- Tempted to keep researching to avoid building → ship something and learn from real usage instead.
- Company is "succeeding" → keep talking to users anyway; this is when teams stop and drift.

### Checklist
- [ ] Reached ~6–8 diverse people per key segment
- [ ] Identified clear, repeated patterns
- [ ] Set up ongoing (not one-time) user contact
- [ ] Avoided using research as a way to delay shipping

### Common Mistakes
- **Treating research as a finished phase.** Why: it feels like a box to check. Better: keep a permanent channel to users; the market moves.
- **Endless research instead of building.** Why: it's safer than shipping. Better: learn enough to act, then test in the real world.

## How do I get my first users?

### Short Answer
Recruit them by hand, one at a time — the first users are impossibly hard to get and it's your job as a founder to do whatever it takes. Don't buy ads; go where your users are, talk to strangers, and do unscalable things to delight your earliest adopters. The boulder is steepest at the start; it gets easier as word of mouth builds.

### Key Principles
- It's your personal responsibility to drag in the first users — 100 emails a day, calls, your network, showing up where they gather.
- Recruit manually, not with ads (Pinterest's founder chatted up people in coffee shops; Stripe and Airbnb signed up users in person).
- Do things that don't scale: this is a startup's biggest early advantage, and you should keep doing it as long as humanly possible.
- Don't give the product away free — people don't value free, and free users don't convert; make them value it.
- The first users are hardest because you have no testimonials and no track record; expect a low conversion rate and push anyway.

### Framework
- No users yet → go to where your customers physically or digitally congregate and recruit one by one.
- Tempted to buy ads for the first users → don't; do it by hand to learn and to build relationships.
- Tempted to give it away to get numbers → charge; paid users give honest feedback and real validation.
- It feels brutally slow → normal; the incline is steepest at the start.

### Checklist
- [ ] You (the founder) are personally recruiting users
- [ ] You're going where your users already are
- [ ] You're doing unscalable, high-touch things to delight them
- [ ] You're charging rather than giving it away
- [ ] You're personally onboarding and following up with each early user

### Common Mistakes
- **Buying ads for your first users.** Why: it feels scalable. Better: recruit by hand; you learn and build loyalty.
- **Giving the product away to inflate numbers.** Why: growth looks good. Better: charge; free users rarely convert and give false signals.
- **Giving up because it's painfully slow.** Why: the start is the hardest part. Better: expect it; persistence compounds.

## How do I turn early users into champions and handle support?

### Short Answer
Do customer support yourself, for as long as humanly possible — it's your richest source of product truth and the way you turn early users into champions. Respond fast, take accountability instead of making excuses, and over-deliver on rescuing bad experiences. Talking to and delighting your first users is unscalable, and that's exactly why it's your advantage.

### Key Principles
- Founders should run support personally in the early days; everyone doing support keeps the whole team close to users (Teespring's founders did all support until ~$130K+/month).
- The four relationship-killers in support are criticism, contempt, defensiveness, and stonewalling — and stonewalling (not responding) is the most common and most lethal.
- Proactively reach out to churned users; sometimes they just need to know you care, and you always learn why they left.
- One delighted early user tells others; one badly-handled user undoes ten champions — so rescue bad experiences aggressively.
- Direct exposure to users correlates with product quality; if you go more than a few weeks without it, the product degrades.

### Framework
- Early stage → founders do support directly; treat each ticket as research.
- A user has a bad experience → bite the bullet, make it fully right, even at cost.
- A user churns → reach out personally to learn why and possibly win them back.
- Support volume is rising → invest in self-serve docs and infrastructure (one doc redesign cut Wufoo's support load ~30%) rather than just hiring bodies.

### Checklist
- [ ] Founders are still doing (or closely reading) support
- [ ] Response times are fast and no message goes ignored
- [ ] You take accountability rather than making excuses
- [ ] Bad experiences are actively rescued
- [ ] You proactively contact churned users
- [ ] You invest in docs/self-serve as volume grows

### Common Mistakes
- **Outsourcing support too early.** Why: it feels like a distraction from building. Better: it *is* building — it's your feedback loop.
- **Going silent under load (stonewalling).** Why: overwhelm. Better: respond even briefly; silence churns users fastest.
- **Treating a botched order as "close enough."** Why: it's easier. Better: make it fully right; rescued customers become champions.

# Growth

## When should I focus on growth?

### Short Answer
Only after you have product-market fit — proven by a flattening retention curve. Running growth tactics before fit is the single most common mistake: a product people don't keep using won't grow no matter how hard you push, and you'll waste money pouring users into a leaky bucket. Until then, the whole company should be focused on building something people love.

### Key Principles
- Product-market fit comes first; growth tactics amplify fit, they don't create it.
- A bad product with great growth marketing is still a bad product — and the growth won't stick.
- The clearest "ready to grow" signal is a cohort retention curve that flattens above zero.
- Before fit, the entire team works on the product, not on growth.
- Once you do grow, momentum becomes self-reinforcing — but only on a foundation of real retention.

### Framework
- Retention curve still sloping to zero → not ready; go back to the product.
- Retention curve flattens above zero → fit achieved; now invest in growth.
- Considering paid acquisition before fit → stop; you'll burn money churning users.

### Checklist
- [ ] Cohort retention curve flattens above zero
- [ ] Organic word-of-mouth is happening
- [ ] You've fixed churn before spending on acquisition
- [ ] The team understands growth amplifies fit, it doesn't replace it

### Common Mistakes
- **Hiring a growth team / buying ads before fit.** Why: growth feels like the goal. Better: earn retention first; growth without it is wasted spend.
- **Mistaking a launch spike for fit.** Why: the numbers look exciting. Better: look at whether cohorts stay.

## Which metrics actually matter?

### Short Answer
Retention above all — it's the single most important thing for growth, because growth is essentially conversion minus churn. Track cohort retention, active usage, and unit economics (lifetime value vs. acquisition cost), and pick one "north star" metric that captures real value delivered. Ignore vanity metrics like total registrations; they feel good and tell you nothing.

### Key Principles
- Retention is the foundation; a 1% churn reduction equals a 1% conversion gain, and churn is usually cheaper to fix.
- Pick a north star that reflects delivered value (Facebook: monthly actives; Airbnb: nights booked; eBay: gross merchandise volume), and align the whole company to it.
- Watch unit economics: lifetime value must exceed customer acquisition cost, and mind the payback period.
- Vanity metrics (total signups, press mentions) mislead; measure active engagement and cohort behavior.
- Most metrics correlate, so choose one north star and don't let teams optimize conflicting numbers.

### Framework
- Choosing what to optimize → pick the single metric that best represents real value to users, and make it the north star.
- Deciding where to improve growth → compare cost of reducing churn vs. raising conversion; usually churn is the cheaper lever.
- Evaluating paid acquisition → require LTV > CAC and a payback period you can survive (3 months is safe; beyond 12 is dangerous).
- A metric looks great but the business isn't → check it's not a vanity metric; return to retention.

### Checklist
- [ ] You track cohort retention as the primary health metric
- [ ] You've chosen one north star metric tied to real value
- [ ] You know your LTV, CAC, and payback period
- [ ] You've stopped reporting vanity metrics
- [ ] The whole company knows and aligns to the north star

### Common Mistakes
- **Optimizing total registrations or press.** Why: the numbers are big and flattering. Better: track active, retained users.
- **Chasing conversion while ignoring churn.** Why: acquisition feels like growth. Better: fix the leak first; it's cheaper.
- **Letting teams optimize different metrics.** Why: no single owner of the number. Better: one north star, repeated relentlessly.

## How should startups acquire users?

### Short Answer
There are three real engines — sticky (retention), viral (referral), and paid (ads) — and you should master one at a time rather than dabbling in all of them. Early on, the most powerful "channel" is doing unscalable things by hand. Be relentlessly creative: find the one tactic in your channel that no one else is doing and push it to the extreme.

### Key Principles
- Three growth types: sticky (deliver an addictive experience so people return), viral (great experience plus excellent referral mechanics), paid (LTV must exceed CAC).
- Focus on one channel at a time; when a channel taps out, drop it — but revisit it later when you're stronger (Homejoy couldn't afford ads early but could later).
- Early on, manual and unscalable acquisition beats any "channel."
- Viral growth needs payload, conversion, and frequency working together — and most products are far less viral than founders assume.
- Creativity is the edge: if growth were easy and bland, everyone would be doing it; find the unique angle.

### Framework
- Choosing a channel → pick the one that fits your product (sticky for habitual use, viral for social products, paid where LTV > CAC).
- Evaluating paid → only scale when LTV > CAC with an acceptable payback period.
- A channel stops working → drop it now, revisit when your economics improve.
- Before any channel → exhaust manual, unscalable acquisition first.

### Checklist
- [ ] Identified which of sticky/viral/paid fits your product
- [ ] Focused on one channel at a time
- [ ] Confirmed paid economics (LTV > CAC, payback period) before scaling
- [ ] Found a creative angle competitors aren't using
- [ ] Still doing unscalable acquisition where it helps

### Common Mistakes
- **Spreading thin across many channels.** Why: hedging feels safe. Better: master one, then add another.
- **Overestimating virality.** Why: it's the dream of free growth. Better: measure the actual referral loop; most products aren't viral.
- **Scaling paid before the math works.** Why: spend feels like growth. Better: prove LTV > CAC and a survivable payback first.

## What is the "magic moment" and north star metric?

### Short Answer
The magic moment is the instant a user first experiences your product's core value (seeing a friend's face on Facebook, finding the right item on eBay, sending a first message on WhatsApp). Everything in growth should be designed to get users to that moment as fast as possible. The north star metric operationalizes it — a single number, like "10 friends in 14 days," that the whole company optimizes.

### Key Principles
- Define the one moment where users "get it," then engineer the funnel to deliver it quickly.
- Operationalize the magic moment into a concrete metric (Facebook's "10 friends in 14 days").
- Optimize for the *marginal* user on the verge of churning, not your power users who are already sold.
- Reaching the magic moment matters more than top-of-funnel signups (eBay shifted incentives from "registered" to "activated" users and growth accelerated even though raw signups fell).
- A single north star aligns the company; without it, teams optimize conflicting things.

### Framework
- Don't know your magic moment → study what retained users do that churned users don't; that delta is it.
- Choosing growth experiments → prioritize anything that gets more users to the magic moment faster.
- Deciding who to optimize for → focus on marginal users, not power users.
- Setting company focus → translate the magic moment into one north star metric everyone shares.

### Checklist
- [ ] You've identified the precise moment users first feel core value
- [ ] You've turned it into a measurable threshold (the north star)
- [ ] Your onboarding is built to reach that moment fast
- [ ] You're optimizing for marginal users, not power users
- [ ] The whole company knows the north star

### Common Mistakes
- **Optimizing for power users.** Why: they're vocal and engaged. Better: power users are already retained; growth comes from converting marginal users.
- **Optimizing signups over activation.** Why: signups are easy to count. Better: optimize reaching the magic moment, even if raw signups drop.

## Should I hire a growth team?

### Short Answer
Not as a startup. The whole company is the growth team, and the CEO is the head of growth. A dedicated growth function makes sense only after you have product-market fit and enough scale for it to be a separate team. Before that, hiring people to "do growth" is a way of avoiding the real work: making the product something people keep using.

### Key Principles
- In a startup, growth is everyone's job and the CEO leads it.
- A dedicated growth team is a post-fit, at-scale luxury, not an early-stage fix (Facebook formed its growth team when it hit a wall around 50M users).
- Hiring for growth before fit papers over a product problem.
- Growth is driven by the product and the magic moment, not by a separate department early on.

### Framework
- Pre product-market fit → no growth team; the whole company focuses on the product.
- Post-fit and at scale → a dedicated growth team can push past plateaus.
- Tempted to hire growth to fix weak traction → fix the product instead.

### Checklist
- [ ] You have product-market fit before considering a growth team
- [ ] The CEO is currently acting as head of growth
- [ ] The whole company treats growth as shared responsibility
- [ ] You're not using a growth hire to mask a product problem

### Common Mistakes
- **Hiring a growth team before fit.** Why: it feels like investing in growth. Better: the whole company owns growth; fix the product first.
- **Abdicating growth to a department.** Why: founders want to focus elsewhere. Better: early on, the CEO leads growth.

# Sales & Marketing

## Do founders need to do sales themselves?

### Short Answer
Yes — selling *is* talking to your users, and that's one of only two things a founder should be doing (the other is building). You can't outsource early sales to a hired salesperson; you are the salesperson. Your passion for the product and deep knowledge of the problem beat professional polish, and doing it yourself teaches you the business.

### Key Principles
- "Talking to users" and "selling" are the same activity; both are core founder work.
- Founder advantages — passion, conviction, deep domain knowledge — outsell smooth professional technique early on.
- Doing sales yourself teaches you the customer, the objections, and the product gaps faster than any report.
- Hiring salespeople before you understand the sale just buries the lesson; learn it first, then scale it.
- Early-stage selling is a numbers game and a grind — expect to do it personally and relentlessly.

### Framework
- Early stage → founders do all sales; treat it as customer research that also brings revenue.
- Tempted to hire a salesperson to avoid selling → don't; you must learn the sale before you can delegate it.
- You've personally run a repeatable sales process → only then hire to scale it.

### Checklist
- [ ] Founders are personally selling and talking to customers
- [ ] You're learning objections and product gaps from each conversation
- [ ] You haven't hired salespeople to avoid doing it yourself
- [ ] You can articulate a repeatable process before scaling it

### Common Mistakes
- **Planning to "hire sales later" instead of doing it now.** Why: founders think they're not salespeople. Better: your passion and knowledge are the advantage — sell yourself.
- **Hiring salespeople before you understand the sale.** Why: it feels like progress. Better: learn the repeatable motion first.

## How do I run an effective sales process and close deals?

### Short Answer
Treat it as a funnel — prospect, have conversations, close, collect revenue — and remember that on calls you should shut up and listen (the best salespeople listen ~70% of the time). Follow up far more than feels reasonable; deals routinely take dozens of touches. Above all, drive every prospect to a clear yes or no — the maybes are what kill you.

### Key Principles
- Listen more than you talk; understand the customer's problem better than they do before pitching.
- Follow-up is unreasonable by normal standards — expect to email, call, and chase many times (one deal took 60+ touches).
- Get to YES or NO, never MAYBE; your time is your only finite resource and maybes drain it.
- Only ~2.5% of any market are true innovators/early adopters — so early sales is a volume game; contact many to find the few.
- Free trials are a trap; counter with an annual agreement plus a short opt-out window to get commitment and a safety net.

### Framework
- On a call → ask why they took it, how they solve this today, what their ideal looks like; listen 70%, talk 30%.
- Prospecting → start with your personal network, then conferences where customers gather, then concise cold emails.
- A prospect is a "maybe" → push for a decision; free your time for real yeses.
- They ask for a free trial → offer an annual deal with a 30–60 day opt-out instead.
- Negotiating the contract → use standard docs, don't over-lawyer minor clauses; sign, get a reference customer, move on.

### Checklist
- [ ] You're treating sales as a funnel with clear stages
- [ ] You listen far more than you talk on calls
- [ ] You follow up well past the point of comfort
- [ ] You convert every prospect to a yes or a no
- [ ] You counter free-trial requests with committed-but-safe terms
- [ ] You use standard contracts and avoid over-negotiating

### Common Mistakes
- **Leading with features.** Why: founders are proud of the product. Better: listen first; understand the problem.
- **Giving up after one or two non-responses.** Why: it feels like rejection. Better: expect dozens of touches; persistence closes deals.
- **Letting maybes linger.** Why: they feel like live opportunities. Better: force a decision; maybes waste your scarcest resource.
- **Over-negotiating contract terms.** Why: fear of a bad clause. Better: sign with standard docs; revenue and references matter more.

## How should I think about pricing?

### Short Answer
Keep it simple and state it with confidence. Simple pricing (per seat, per month) wins; complex pricing signals you don't understand your own business model. Match your pricing to your sales model — high-touch sales only works at high price points; low prices demand self-serve. And don't hedge: "we charge $10K a month" beats "maybe $10–15K, we're flexible."

### Key Principles
- Simple pricing wins; complexity signals you don't know your business model.
- State your price plainly and confidently; hedging signals weakness and uncertainty.
- Pricing and sales model must match — "elephants" (few customers, ~$100K) justify high-touch sales; "mice" (many customers, ~$1K) require self-serve.
- If your target is mid-market ("rabbits"), you can't afford high-touch sales per customer — rethink pricing or go self-serve.
- Charging real money is also validation; it forces honest demand signals.

### Framework
- Selling few, expensive deals → high-touch, founder-led sales is viable.
- Selling many, cheap deals → you need self-serve/low-touch; high-touch won't pay for itself.
- Pricing feels complicated → simplify it until you can say it in one sentence.
- Asked your price → state one clear number, don't hedge.

### Checklist
- [ ] Pricing is simple enough to state in one sentence
- [ ] You say it confidently, without hedging
- [ ] Your price point matches your sales model (high-touch vs. self-serve)
- [ ] You're charging real money, not relying on free

### Common Mistakes
- **Overcomplicating pricing.** Why: trying to capture every case. Better: simple pricing; complexity reads as not knowing your model.
- **Hedging on price.** Why: fear of scaring the customer. Better: state one number confidently.
- **Mismatching price and sales motion.** Why: not thinking through unit economics. Better: align price point to whether you can afford high-touch sales.

## Should I pursue press and PR?

### Short Answer
Usually not as a growth strategy — press is a vanity metric that feels like success without proving you make money, get users, or keep them happy. It can help land your first few hundred customers if you have a clear goal and target the right outlet, but it doesn't scale. ### Key Principles

- Target the outlet your audience reads, not the most prestigious one (Exec targeted the local SF paper; Twitch targeted gaming blogs).
- PR firms can supply contacts and logistics but can't tell you what's interesting — learn to pitch yourself first, especially early.
- Treat press as a sales funnel: warm intros (best from founders a reporter just covered) beat cold emails; make your story genuinely original.

### Framework
- Considering press → first define the audience and goal; if unclear, don't pursue it.
- Goal is customers in a niche → target the niche/local/industry outlet, not national tech press.
- Tempted to hire a PR firm early → learn to do it yourself first; firms are expensive and can't find your story.
- Pitching reporters → get a warm intro, give lead time, and lead with what's genuinely new.

- [ ] You have a clear audience and goal for any press
- [ ] You're targeting the outlet your audience actually reads
- [ ] Your story is original, not a rehash
- [ ] You're getting warm intros to reporters
- [ ] You're not relying on press as a scalable growth channel

- **Hiring a PR firm before you can pitch.** Why: it feels professional. Better: learn the story and the process yourself first.
- **Targeting prestige over fit.** Why: big outlets feel like bigger wins. Better: reach the outlet your customers read.

## How do I handle customer feature requests in sales?

### Short Answer
Be very skeptical of "I'd buy it if it had feature X." Nine times out of ten, you build X and the customer finds another reason not to buy. If they're a must-win, make the feature a signed contract term so they're committed too; otherwise, wait until several customers ask for the same thing before building it.

### Key Principles
- "I'd use it if it had X" almost never converts — there's usually a next objection waiting.
- If the customer truly matters, tie the feature to a signed contract so commitment is mutual.
- Wait for a pattern: build a requested feature only when multiple customers independently ask.
- Building one-off features for a single customer slows your velocity and rarely lands the deal.
- Distinguish the request from the underlying problem — the real need may be solvable another way.

### Framework
- One customer demands a feature to buy → get them to sign a contract contingent on delivery, or pass.
- Several customers ask for the same feature → now it's a real signal; build it.
- A request comes in → probe the underlying problem before committing to the literal feature.

### Checklist
- [ ] You don't build features on a single customer's promise alone
- [ ] Must-win features are tied to signed commitments
- [ ] You wait for multiple requests before building
- [ ] You've traced each request to the real underlying problem

### Common Mistakes
- **Building a custom feature to win one deal.** Why: the deal feels close. Better: nine times out of ten another objection appears — require commitment or wait for a pattern.
- **Treating every request as a roadmap item.** Why: wanting to please. Better: build on patterns, not one-offs.

# Fundraising

## Should I bootstrap or raise money?

### Short Answer
Bootstrap as long as you can — investors love businesses that don't need their money, and a working business makes capital easy to raise. Roughly 95% of startups can get a product to market with minimal capital, so never let "we can't launch without funding" be your excuse. Raise money to peel away specific risks and accelerate, not as a milestone or a measure of success.

### Key Principles
- Bootstrap for as long as possible; not needing capital is the strongest fundraising position.
- Fundraising is not success and not a milestone — it just puts you in position to do the harder things.
- Most startups can reach market with little capital; don't make investors the gating factor.
- Raise to eliminate specific risks (the "onion" of risks peels layer by layer), not to hoard cash.
- A working business attracts capital effortlessly; a pitch can't substitute for momentum.

### Framework
- Business is working and growing → capital is easy; raise to accelerate if it helps.
- Business isn't working yet → don't fundraise; fix the business first (raising will be hard and shouldn't be the priority).
- Can you reach market with little money? → almost always yes; do that before raising.
- Deciding how much → tie it to the specific risks this round will eliminate.

### Checklist
- [ ] You've bootstrapped as far as practical
- [ ] You're raising to peel specific risks, not to hit a milestone
- [ ] You haven't let funding gate your launch
- [ ] Your business shows momentum before you pitch

### Common Mistakes
- **Treating fundraising as success.** Why: it's celebrated publicly. Better: it just enables harder work ahead.
- **Raising before the business works.** Why: hoping money fixes it. Better: fix the business; then capital comes easily.
- **Letting lack of funding excuse not launching.** Why: it feels like a real blocker. Better: most products reach market cheaply.

## When is the right time to fundraise?

### Short Answer
When you're strong — when investors are chasing you, not when you're desperate. The best time is after you have traction (launched, growing ~30% month-over-month, real users or revenue). If you must raise pre-traction, sell extreme execution speed and a fully committed team. Run the whole process in parallel, in a tight window, with one founder leading it so the company keeps building.

### Key Principles
- Raise from a position of strength; if investors aren't trying to give you money, you're weak — fix that first.
- Traction (launch + ~30% monthly growth + users/revenue) is the strongest position.
- Pre-traction, substitute proof of speed (8 months of work in 2) and total team commitment.
- Run meetings in parallel in a single sprint; signal "we're busy building, available in week 3" so calendars open and you conserve energy.
- Put one founder on fundraising full-time; don't let it distract the whole company.

### Framework
- You have traction → raise now; you have leverage.
- No traction but extreme speed + committed full-time team → you can raise on that narrative.
- Investors aren't reaching out → don't push a raise; build traction or generate genuine interest first.
- Running the process → batch all meetings into one tight window, one founder leading.

### Checklist
- [ ] You're raising from strength, not desperation
- [ ] You have traction, or a credible speed/commitment story
- [ ] Meetings are batched into a tight parallel sprint
- [ ] One founder leads the raise; the rest keep building

### Common Mistakes
- **Raising when desperate.** Why: you're out of cash. Better: raise when strong; build the strength first.
- **Sequential meetings dragging for months.** Why: taking them as they come. Better: parallelize into a sprint to create urgency and protect focus.
- **The whole team fundraising.** Why: it feels all-hands important. Better: one founder owns it; the company keeps shipping.

## What do investors care about most?

### Short Answer
The founders, above everything. Investors decide in the first minutes whether you're a leader and whether you're obsessed with your product — and venture is a game of extreme outliers, so they invest in unusual strength, not the absence of weakness. The best thing you can do isn't a better pitch; it's a better business: "be so good they can't ignore you."

### Key Principles
- Investors read leadership and product obsession almost immediately.
- Venture returns come from a handful of extreme outliers, so investors back exceptional strength and tolerate real flaws.
- The single best fundraising move is making the business undeniable, not polishing the deck.
- A team that's clearly executing — getting things done every time you meet them — is the strongest predictor of success.
- The quality of the founding team is the foundation everything else rests on.

### Framework
- Improving your odds → make the business better, not just the pitch ("be so good they can't ignore you").
- They're evaluating you → demonstrate leadership, product obsession, and decisiveness.
- You have a serious weakness → lean into an exceptional strength; outliers win despite flaws.
- They're checking traction → show consistent execution and momentum since the last conversation.

- [ ] You can show leadership and deep product obsession
- [ ] You've made the business itself compelling, not just the pitch

## How much should I raise and at what valuation and dilution?

### Short Answer
Raise the amount that peels away the specific risks of your next stage — not the most you can get. Watch dilution carefully across rounds: roughly 10–15% in a seed and 20–30% in a Series A is normal, and giving away more than ~40% early can doom your cap table. Hitting an investable valuation threshold matters more than maximizing the headline number.

### Key Principles
- Size the round to the risks it eliminates, not to "as much as possible."
- Typical dilution: ~10–15% at seed, ~20–30% at Series A; chronic over-dilution kills founder motivation.
- Giving away more than ~40% in the angel/seed round can doom you — you'll own too little after later rounds.
- Valuation has thresholds: investors balk above a line and pile in below it (one founder went from universal rejection at a $12–15M cap to oversubscribed at $9M).
- Don't raise more than you need just because you can; excess capital invites waste and dilution.

### Framework
- Setting the round size → list the risks this stage must eliminate; raise exactly enough to do that.
- Setting valuation → find the threshold where demand appears; hitting it beats a higher number with no takers.
- Cumulative dilution looks high → pull back; protect founder ownership across the full round sequence.
- Demand exceeds your target → resist raising much more than you need.

### Checklist
- [ ] Round size is tied to specific risks it removes
- [ ] Seed dilution is roughly 10–15%, Series A roughly 20–30%
- [ ] You're not giving away more than ~40% early
- [ ] You've found an investable valuation threshold
- [ ] You're not raising materially more than needed

### Common Mistakes
- **Raising the maximum possible.** Why: more cash feels safer. Better: raise to peel risks; excess invites waste and dilution.
- **Over-diluting early.** Why: chasing a big round. Better: protect ownership; >40% early can doom the cap table.
- **Maximizing valuation over fundability.** Why: ego and headlines. Better: hit the threshold where money actually shows up.

## How do I pitch effectively?

### Short Answer
Get it down to a crisp 30-second and 2-minute version, and practice until it's muscle memory. The 30-second pitch: what you do (in one plain sentence that passes the mom test), market size, and traction. The 2-minute version adds your unique insight, how you make money, the team, and a specific ask. Anything longer just gives investors room to say no.

### Key Principles
- A clear one-sentence description of what you do is non-negotiable — 25% of founders can't even do this.
- 30-second pitch = what you do + market size + traction.
- 2-minute pitch adds a surprising unique insight, clear monetization, the team, and the ask.
- State monetization and the ask directly ("we charge $10K/month"; "raising $500K on an $8.5M cap") — vagueness signals you don't know your business.
- Ask for what you want; most of the time you won't be laughed out of the room.

### Framework
- Investor says "tell me about your company" → deliver the 30-second pitch from memory.
- They lean in → expand to the 2-minute version: insight, monetization, team, ask.
- Explaining monetization → say one clear number, not "maybe ads, maybe subscriptions."
- Closing → state the specific ask (amount, instrument, cap, minimum check).

### Checklist
- [ ] You can say what you do in one mom-test sentence
- [ ] You've rehearsed a 30-second pitch (what / market / traction)
- [ ] You've rehearsed a 2-minute pitch (insight / money / team / ask)
- [ ] Your monetization is one clear statement
- [ ] Your ask is specific and direct

### Common Mistakes
- **Rambling for ten minutes.** Why: wanting to convey everything. Better: short pitches; long ones invite objections.
- **Vague monetization.** Why: not having decided. Better: pick one model and state it plainly.
- **Not making a clear ask.** Why: fear of presumption. Better: ask directly; you usually won't be refused for asking.

## How do I choose investors and handle terms and board seats?

### Short Answer
Pick investors like you'd pick a spouse — it's a 15–20 year relationship that matters more than valuation or terms. Take money from people with relevant expertise, useful connections, and genuine respect for you; walk away from those motivated only by the check. Be cautious with board seats and protective terms, and never take money from unsophisticated investors who'll panic when things get hard.

### Key Principles
- Investor choice is a long-term partnership; weight the person over the terms.
- Take capital from investors who add expertise and connections, not just money.
- Generally decline board seats unless the investor adds real strategic value beyond capital.
- Real power lives in protective covenants and the relationship, not in board votes — so pick the right partners.
- Avoid unsophisticated money (the uncle, the small check) — they don't understand startup risk and become liabilities when the company isn't exciting.

### Framework
- Investor only brings money → think hard; money alone is not rare.
- Investor brings expertise, network, and respect → strong yes.
- They want a board seat → grant it only if they add operational/strategic value.
- Offered money by unsophisticated investors → decline; they'll panic and create problems later.
- They ask for an NDA or extra shares "for advice" → red flags; reconsider.

### Checklist
- [ ] You'd be glad to work with this investor for 15+ years
- [ ] They add expertise and connections, not just capital
- [ ] You're selective about board seats
- [ ] You're avoiding unsophisticated/easily-spooked money
- [ ] You're wary of NDA requests and extra-share grabs

### Common Mistakes
- **Optimizing for valuation over partner quality.** Why: the number is visible and ego-flattering. Better: the relationship outlasts the terms.
- **Taking money from anyone who offers.** Why: cash is tempting. Better: unsophisticated investors become liabilities in hard times.
- **Giving board seats for capital alone.** Why: it seems standard. Better: reserve seats for real strategic value.

## Is enterprise or consumer a better bet?

### Short Answer
Don't dismiss enterprise as boring — the economics are often far better. Enterprise IT is a multi-trillion-dollar market versus a much smaller consumer one, and cloud, mobile, and user-led adoption have made enterprise startups faster and more viable than ever. The winning move in enterprise is a wedge: solve one sliver of a problem incredibly well, then expand, rather than fighting incumbents head-on.

### Key Principles
- Enterprise IT spend dwarfs the consumer opportunity; weigh market × monetization, not "fun."
- Cloud, standardized platforms, and mobile have made enterprise faster to build and sell than it used to be.
- Mobile flipped the model from IT-gatekeeper to user-led: sell to end users first, then roll up to IT — which favors startups with no incumbent relationship.
- Win with a wedge: be best-in-class at one slice incumbents won't optimize, then expand up-market and across use cases.
- Find asymmetries — technical, economic, or business-model — that incumbents can't or won't copy (e.g., Zenefits monetizing via insurance commissions, not customer fees).

### Framework
- Choosing a market → multiply realistic TAM by monetization feasibility; enterprise often wins on economics.
- Entering against an incumbent → don't compete on their full feature set; find a wedge and own it.
- Looking for an opening → exploit cloud/mobile shifts and user-led adoption.
- Designing the business → find an asymmetry incumbents structurally can't match.

### Checklist
- [ ] You've compared markets on TAM × monetization, not appeal
- [ ] You've identified a wedge you can dominate
- [ ] You're exploiting user-led adoption rather than fighting IT gatekeepers
- [ ] You've found an asymmetry incumbents can't easily copy

### Common Mistakes
- **Dismissing enterprise as unsexy.** Why: consumer feels more exciting. Better: enterprise economics are often far stronger.
- **Attacking an incumbent's full feature set.** Why: trying to match them. Better: win a wedge, then expand.

## How do I choose a cofounder?

### Short Answer
Treat it as one of the most important decisions of your life — comparable to marriage — and never pick a near-stranger. Choose someone you've known for years, whom you trust deeply, who is relentlessly resourceful, and whose skills complement yours. A bad cofounder is worse than no cofounder, but for the biggest companies, two or three founders beats going solo.

### Key Principles
- Cofounder choice is marriage-level; the history between you is what holds you together when things go wrong.
- Never pick a cofounder you just met — the track record of strangers-as-cofounders is disastrous (of YC teams that added a cofounder right before starting, all fell apart within a year).
- You want "James Bond" resourcefulness — unflappable, tough, decisive, creative — more than narrow domain expertise.
- Two or three cofounders is the sweet spot; solo is harder and rarely builds the biggest companies.
- Trust your gut on people; ignoring a bad feeling about someone impressive is a classic, costly mistake.

### Framework
- Known them well for years → best.
- Met in college or worked together at a serious company → good.
- Random person from a cofounder-matching event → don't.
- Considering going solo → acceptable, but harder; better than a bad cofounder.
- Skills overlap completely → reconsider; you want complementary strengths (especially a technical cofounder for software).

### Checklist
- [ ] You've known your cofounder for years
- [ ] You deeply trust them and have history under stress
- [ ] Their skills complement yours (technical cofounder for software)
- [ ] They're resourceful, tough, calm, and decisive
- [ ] You'd choose them as a friend, and your gut is at ease
- [ ] You're at two or three founders, not one or five

### Common Mistakes
- **Picking a cofounder you barely know.** Why: urgency to start. Better: the track record is terrible; wait for someone with real history.
- **Ignoring a bad gut feeling about an impressive person.** Why: credentials override instinct. Better: trust your read on people.
- **Choosing for overlapping skills.** Why: comfort and similarity. Better: complement each other.

## How should we split equity among founders?

### Short Answer
Split it roughly equally, and put everyone on vesting (the standard is four years with a one-year cliff). Equal-ish equity signals equal commitment; if you're unwilling to give a cofounder a near-equal share, question whether they should be a cofounder at all. Don't over-reward the "idea person" — execution creates value, ideas alone are worth nearly nothing.

### Key Principles
- Near-equal splits signal everyone is fully committed for the long haul; lopsided splits surface hidden doubts.
- Always use vesting (4 years, 1-year cliff) — even solo founders — so an early departure doesn't leave dead equity on the cap table.
- Execution, not ideas, creates value; over-weighting the idea person is a red flag investors notice.
- Discuss and document equity early — don't let it fester.
- Top companies show essentially zero cases of wildly disproportionate founder splits.

### Framework
- Unwilling to give a near-equal share → reconsider whether they're really a cofounder.
- Tempted to give the idea person extra → don't; execution is where value comes from.
- Setting terms → 4-year vesting, 1-year cliff, for everyone including solo founders.
- A founder leaves early → vesting ensures the company recovers the unvested shares.

### Checklist
- [ ] Founder equity is split roughly equally
- [ ] Everyone (including solo founders) is on a 4-year vest with a 1-year cliff
- [ ] You haven't over-rewarded the idea person
- [ ] Equity is documented early, not left vague

### Common Mistakes
- **Skipping vesting.** Why: it feels distrustful among friends. Better: vesting protects everyone and the cap table; investors require it.
- **Over-paying the idea person.** Why: "it was my idea." Better: ideas are cheap; execution creates value.
- **Leaving equity undiscussed.** Why: avoiding an awkward conversation. Better: settle it early before resentment builds.

## When should I hire my first employee?

### Short Answer
As late as you possibly can — be proud of how few employees you have. Early hires carry enormous risk: a single mediocre hire in your first few people can kill the company. When you finally do hire, make it your number one priority, take months if needed, and only bring someone on if you'd bet the company's future on them.

### Key Principles
- Try not to hire; small teams move faster, burn less, and avoid the damage of a bad early hire.
- A mediocre hire among your first five can poison the culture and kill the startup (a mediocre hire at a big company is survivable; at a startup it's fatal).
- When you do hire, it becomes your #1 priority — recruiting great people takes up to a year (Airbnb took ~5 months to make its first hire).
- The bar for each early hire: "Would I bet the future of the company on this single person?"
- Later, once it's working, you can hire fast and scale — but not in the first handful of people.

### Framework
- Can you avoid hiring? → do; stay small as long as possible.
- You genuinely, desperately need someone → make hiring your top priority and take the time to get it right.
- Unsure about a candidate → don't hire; the cost of a bad early hire far exceeds being understaffed.
- Past product-market fit and scaling → now you can hire faster.

### Checklist
- [ ] You've confirmed you genuinely can't avoid hiring
- [ ] Hiring is currently your #1 priority
- [ ] You'd bet the company's future on this person
- [ ] You've given the search the months it needs
- [ ] You're not compromising out of impatience

### Common Mistakes
- **Hiring too early to feel like a "real" company.** Why: headcount feels like progress. Better: stay small; bad early hires are lethal.
- **Compromising on an early hire.** Why: desperation to fill a role. Better: wait; a mediocre hire in the first five can kill you.

## What traits matter most when hiring?

### Short Answer
For early hires, aptitude, belief in the mission, and raw quality matter more than experience — most of the best early hires have never done the exact job before. Look for people who are smart, get things done, communicate well, take risk, and have maniacal determination. Use the three-question test: Are they smart? Do they get things done? Do I want to spend a lot of time around them?

### Key Principles
- Experience matters less than aptitude and belief for early hires (the exception is a role running a large part of the org).
- The three questions: smart, gets things done, and someone you want to be around — yes to all three rarely disappoints.
- Communication skill correlates strongly with hires that work out; hard-to-talk-to people are a real problem.
- Look for risk-takers with maniacal determination, not people choosing between McKinsey and your startup.
- Hire people who believe in the mission; the smartest engineer who doesn't believe won't pour their heart in.

### Framework
- Early-stage hire → weight aptitude, determination, and mission-belief over a polished résumé.
- Candidate can't communicate clearly → serious red flag.
- Role runs a large part of the org → now experience matters more.
- Applying the test → require yes to all three (smart / gets things done / want to be around them).

### Checklist
- [ ] Candidate is clearly smart
- [ ] Candidate has a track record of getting things done
- [ ] You genuinely want to spend lots of time around them
- [ ] They communicate clearly
- [ ] They believe in the mission and take real risk

### Common Mistakes
- **Over-indexing on experience for early hires.** Why: résumés feel safe. Better: aptitude and belief predict early-hire success better.
- **Overlooking "tough" and "calm" because "smart" is obvious.** Why: intelligence is easy to screen for. Better: weigh resilience and composure too.
- **Hiring someone hard to communicate with.** Why: impressive on paper. Better: communication problems compound fast.

## How should founders evaluate candidates?

### Short Answer
Work with someone before you hire them — most first-time founders are bad interviewers but good at judging people after a day or two of real work together. Ask about specific past projects rather than brainteasers, and press references hard: "Is this person in the top 5% you've worked with? Would you hire them again? Why aren't you trying to right now?" For early hires, references are also a great source of more great candidates.

### Key Principles
- Do a real project together (a day or two) before hiring — you both learn far more than in interviews.
- Ask about concrete past work, not puzzles; dig deep into what they actually did.
- Reference checks are high-signal if you push: ask for top-percentile rankings and why they're not rehiring the person.
- The Zuckerberg test: would you be comfortable working for this person if roles were reversed? At minimum, deeply respect them.
- References generously point you to other strong people — use them as a recruiting source.

### Framework
- Possible to work together first → do it; it beats any interview.
- In the interview → ask about specific projects, press into details, avoid brainteasers.
- Checking references → ask for top 1/5/10% rankings and why they aren't rehiring them.
- Evaluating fit → would you respect (or report to) this person? If not, pass.

### Checklist
- [ ] You worked on a real project together before deciding
- [ ] You asked about specific past projects, not puzzles
- [ ] You pushed references for percentile rankings and rehire intent
- [ ] You'd respect (or work for) this person
- [ ] You mined references for other strong candidates

### Common Mistakes
- **Relying on interviews alone.** Why: it's the default. Better: work together; founders judge better after real work.
- **Using brainteasers.** Why: they feel like rigor. Better: probe real past projects.
- **Doing soft reference checks.** Why: discomfort pressing. Better: ask the pointed questions that reveal the truth.

## How generous should I be with employee equity?

## When and how should I fire someone?

### Short Answer
Fire fast when it's genuinely not working — it's better for the company and ultimately for the person — but reserve it for a clear pattern, not one or two mistakes. Also fire people who create office politics or are persistently negative; that toxicity is a huge drag. When you do it, be clear and humane: take the job, not the dignity.

### Key Principles
- Fire fast once it's truly not working; waiting too long is the near-universal first-time founder mistake.
- The bar is a sustained pattern (weeks of getting nearly every decision wrong), not isolated mistakes — early employees should feel secure.
- Also fire for office politics and persistent negativity; it's toxic and contagious.
- Be clear and humane in the conversation: don't ramble or apologize away the decision; preserve dignity.
- Execute the mechanics cleanly: pay all owed wages immediately, cut access, and repurchase unvested shares.

### Framework
- One or two mistakes → not a firing; everyone errs.
- A sustained pattern of getting it wrong → fire fast; it's better for everyone.
- Someone creates politics or is persistently negative → remove them; toxicity spreads.
- Doing the firing → be clear ("we're letting you go"), pay immediately, cut access, repurchase unvested shares, preserve dignity.

### Checklist
- [ ] You're acting on a pattern, not a one-off mistake
- [ ] You're firing fast once it's clearly not working
- [ ] You remove political/persistently-negative people
- [ ] You communicate clearly and preserve dignity
- [ ] You pay owed wages immediately and cut access
- [ ] You repurchase unvested shares

### Common Mistakes
- **Waiting too long to fire.** Why: it's painful and awful. Better: fire fast; it's better for the company and the person.
- **Firing over one or two mistakes.** Why: overreaction. Better: act on patterns; keep early employees feeling secure.
- **Mushy, unclear firing conversations.** Why: discomfort. Better: be honest and clear while preserving dignity.

## When should I hire senior or experienced executives?

### Short Answer
Around 20–25 employees, when your job shifts from building a great product to building a great company. The #1 management mistake at scale is being afraid to hire experienced executives who've built companies before — nearly every founder says they wish they'd done it earlier. Inexperienced hustle is fine in the first ten people; at scale you need people who've done it.

### Key Principles
- Early on, aptitude and hustle beat pedigree; at scale (25+), you need executives who've built companies before.
- Fear of hiring senior people is the most common management mistake as you grow.
- Almost every founder regrets not hiring great executives earlier.
- The transition happens around 20–25 people — the same point your job changes from product to company.

- Approaching 20–25 people → start recruiting experienced executives to unblock the org.

# Culture

## What is culture and why does it matter?

### Short Answer
Culture is your core values plus the daily actions of everyone pursuing the mission — not a poster on the wall, but how people actually behave. It matters because it's how the company makes decisions when you're not in the room, and strong cultures measurably outperform. Crucially, culture is set by what the founders *do*: whatever you model becomes the culture.

### Key Principles
- Culture = core values + the daily behavior of every team member in pursuit of the mission.
- It's a framework for decentralized decision-making — the invariant you keep as decisions spread beyond you.
- Everything gets modeled after the founders; whatever you do becomes the culture, and you can't outsource it.
- Strong cultures have real economic power (firms known for great culture have outperformed the market over decades).
- Culture is a daily habit, like fitness — not a one-time crash diet.

### Framework
- Decisions are scaling beyond you → culture is how they stay aligned; invest in it deliberately.
- Wondering where culture comes from → look at your own behavior; you are modeling it.
- Tempted to delegate culture → don't; it flows from the founders.
- Treating culture as a one-time exercise → make it a daily practice instead.

### Checklist
- [ ] Your values are expressed as actual behaviors, not slogans
- [ ] Founders model the culture they want
- [ ] Culture is reinforced daily, not set once
- [ ] You understand culture as your decision-making framework at scale

### Common Mistakes
- **Treating culture as posters and perks.** Why: it's easy and visible. Better: culture is behavior, especially the founders'.
- **Delegating culture.** Why: it feels like an HR task. Better: it flows from what founders do.
- **Crash-dieting culture.** Why: doing it once feels like enough. Better: practice it daily.

## When and how do I define company values?

### Short Answer
Earlier than you think — ideally before you hire anyone, not at 100 employees. Values must be credible and uniquely tied to your mission; generic ones like "honesty" and "teamwork" are useless because everyone claims them. Take the time to make them specific and real, because your first hires set the DNA that every future hire inherits.

### Key Principles
- Define values early; founders who wait until ~100 employees regret it (the first engineer is a "DNA chip" — there will be a thousand people like them).
- Generic values are worthless; they must be unique, credible, and tied to your mission.
- The best values come from your real story (Airbnb's "be a cereal entrepreneur" came from literally selling cereal to survive).
- Interview for values and mission-belief, not just competence.
- Diversity of background is good; diversity of *values* is not — you want shared beliefs, varied perspectives.

### Framework
- Before your first hire → write down your values; it forces clarity before momentum sets in.
- Drafting a value → if any company could claim it, it's too generic; tie it to your specific mission/story.
- Interviewing → assess belief in the mission alongside skill.
- Building the team → seek diverse backgrounds but shared values.

### Checklist
- [ ] Values are written before or very early in hiring
- [ ] Each value is specific and tied to your mission
- [ ] Values come from your real story, not a template
- [ ] You interview for values and mission-belief
- [ ] You seek diverse backgrounds but shared values

### Common Mistakes
- **Waiting until ~100 employees.** Why: it doesn't feel urgent. Better: the first hires set the DNA; define values before hiring.
- **Generic values.** Why: they sound safe and universal. Better: make them unique and credible.
- **Hiring for skill while ignoring mission-belief.** Why: competence is measurable. Better: belief drives whether they pour their heart in.

## How do I keep culture as I scale?

### Short Answer
Hire relentlessly for values, write down how and why you do things, and reinforce culture through what you celebrate and how you make decisions. Culture is gardening, not architecture — plant the right people, pull the weeds, and keep tending it. Around the first dozen hires especially, every person multiplies, so guard the bar.

### Key Principles
- Culture is gardening: plant the right people (hire for values), pull weeds (remove misaligned people), keep tending.
- The first ~10 hires are the most consequential — each brings ~10 more like them.
- Write down how you do things (processes) and why (values) so new hires inherit culture instead of inventing it by accident.
- Four levers: who you hire, what you do daily, what you communicate, and what you celebrate.

### Framework
- Hiring at scale → keep hiring for values; one mediocre culture-fit hire compounds.

- Reinforcing culture → celebrate the behaviors you want; what you celebrate becomes the norm.
- Spotting a poor culture fit → check authenticity of mission-belief, not just enthusiasm.

## How transparent should I be with my team?

### Short Answer
Very — transparency keeps everyone aligned and "forgives a lot of the things that break as you grow" — but it needs structure and norms to work at scale. # Leadership & Management

## What is the CEO's actual job?

### Short Answer
At the highest level, to maximize the output of the organization — and the parts only you can do are: set the vision, raise money, evangelize the mission (recruiting, partners, press), hire and manage, and set the execution bar. The last one is the most critical and least glamorous: the standard you model becomes the company's standard. Much of the job is unglamorous, like being a "$10-an-hour TaskRabbit" for your team.

### Key Principles
- The CEO's one job is to maximize the organization's output (people + tools + environment).
- The five irreducible CEO jobs: set vision, raise money, evangelize, hire and manage, and set the execution bar.
- You set the execution bar by modeling it — you can't hire a COO to do it while you go to conferences.
- A useful metaphor is the editor: simplify, ask clarifying questions, allocate resources, and ensure one consistent voice across everything.
- Get the details right — the small things (the receptionist, the office, the food) cascade into excellence everywhere.

### Framework
- Deciding what only you can do → vision, fundraising, evangelism, hiring/managing, execution bar; delegate the rest.
- Setting the execution bar → model it personally; the team mirrors the founders.
- Leading the company's "voice" → edit for consistency across product, website, recruiting, and support.
- Tempted to outsource culture/standards → don't; these flow from you.

### Checklist
- [ ] You're focused on maximizing org output, not personal output
- [ ] You personally own vision, fundraising, evangelism, hiring, and the execution bar
- [ ] You model the standard rather than delegating it
- [ ] You enforce a consistent voice across everything
- [ ] You sweat the small details that cascade

### Common Mistakes
- **Delegating the execution bar/culture.** Why: it's tempting to hand off. Better: you set it by modeling; it can't be outsourced.
- **Confusing motion with progress.** Why: effort feels like output. Better: measure organizational output, not activity.
- **Ignoring details as beneath you.** Why: they seem minor. Better: small things cascade into company-wide excellence.

## How should founders make decisions and delegate?

### Short Answer
Be decisive — indecisiveness is a startup killer — and delegate by handing over real authority, not by collecting data. Real delegation sounds like "you're smart, here's what I think, you decide, tell me what you chose," not "go research this and report back so I can decide." Match your management style to each person's experience with the task: there's no single right style.

### Key Principles
- Decisiveness matters enormously; bias toward action, and explain your thinking rather than pulling rank.
- Real delegation transfers the decision, not just the legwork — 99.9% of founders aren't Steve Jobs and can't decide everything.
- Match style to task-relevant maturity: how many times has this person done this exact thing? More maturity, less oversight.
- There's no single management style; if half your references call you a micromanager and half a delegator, you're probably doing it right.
- Use a conviction/consequence grid: you decide the high-conviction, high-consequence calls; delegate the low-consequence ones and let people learn (even fail) safely.

### Framework
- High conviction + high consequence → you decide; explain your reasoning.
- Low conviction + low consequence → delegate fully; let them learn, even by failing.
- Delegating a real decision → "here's my view, you decide, tell me what you chose," not "report back and I'll decide."
- Person has done this many times → step back; new to it → coach closely.

### Checklist
- [ ] You make decisions quickly and explain your reasoning
- [ ] You delegate real authority, not just research
- [ ] You vary your style by each person's task experience
- [ ] You reserve high-consequence, high-conviction calls for yourself
- [ ] You let people own (and sometimes fail at) low-stakes decisions

### Common Mistakes
- **Indecisiveness.** Why: fear of being wrong. Better: bias to action; it's a startup killer otherwise.
- **Fake delegation ("research and report back").** Why: it feels safe. Better: transfer the decision and trust them.
- **One management style for everyone.** Why: consistency feels fair. Better: match style to each person's task maturity.

## What should founders spend their time on?

### Short Answer
Identify the two or three things that actually matter each day and ruthlessly ignore, delegate, or defer the rest — the trick to great execution is saying no 97 times out of 100. Audit where your time actually goes versus your stated priorities; founders claim recruiting is the #1 priority yet often spend under 10% of their time on it. Whatever mode you're in (product, hiring, fundraising), that thing should be your single top priority.

### Key Principles
- A hundred important things compete for attention daily; pick the right two or three and ignore the rest.
- Say no roughly 97 times out of 100; this takes conscious, constant effort.
- You get no credit for working hard on the wrong things — only for making something the market wants.
- Whatever mode you're in (product, hiring, fundraising) is your #1 priority, with little in between (e.g., hiring is 0% or 25% of your time, not 10%).
- Do a calendar audit: compare where your time actually goes to your stated priorities; they rarely match.

### Framework
- Facing a hundred demands → choose the two or three that matter; defer/delegate/ignore the rest.
- A new request arrives → default to no unless it's one of your few priorities.
- In hiring mode → it's your #1 priority (≈25% of time), not a side activity.
- Unsure if you're focused → audit your calendar against your stated priorities.

### Checklist
- [ ] You've identified the 2–3 things that matter most right now
- [ ] You say no to almost everything else
- [ ] Your current "mode" is genuinely your top priority
- [ ] You've audited calendar vs. stated priorities
- [ ] You're working hard on the right things, not just hard

### Common Mistakes
- **Working hard on the wrong things.** Why: effort feels virtuous. Better: you only get points for what the market wants.
- **Saying yes too often.** Why: every request seems important. Better: say no ~97% of the time.
- **Claiming a priority you don't fund with time.** Why: intentions feel like action. Better: audit and align your calendar.

## How do I manage people well day to day?

### Short Answer
Do regular one-on-ones (weekly is ideal, never less than every two weeks), with the employee setting the agenda since the meeting is for them. Give clear, direct feedback — but don't tell people they're failing every day or they'll leave. Let your team take credit for wins and take the blame for losses yourself, and keep the people who poison morale out. Learning even a little management goes a long way.

### Key Principles
- Hold one-on-ones every one to two weeks; the employee sets the agenda because the benefit is theirs.
- Limit yourself to ~5–7 direct reports — that's the most you can do real one-on-ones with.
- Give clear feedback, but don't constantly tell people they're messing up; you'll drive them out.
- Praise the team for wins; take responsibility for losses yourself.
- Motivate with autonomy, mastery, and purpose; give people small, growing areas of responsibility instead of micromanaging.

### Framework
- Scheduling → one-on-ones at least every two weeks, weekly if possible, employee-driven agenda.
- More than ~7 reports → restructure; you can't manage them well.
- Giving feedback → be clear and direct, but don't make it a daily barrage of criticism.
- Something went wrong → take the blame publicly; something went right → give the credit away.

### Checklist
- [ ] One-on-ones happen every 1–2 weeks
- [ ] The employee sets the agenda
- [ ] You have ~5–7 direct reports, not more
- [ ] You give clear feedback without constant criticism
- [ ] You credit the team and absorb the blame
- [ ] You give people growing areas of ownership

### Common Mistakes
- **Skipping or irregular one-on-ones.** Why: they feel low-priority. Better: hold them reliably; they're how people grow and stay.
- **Telling people they're failing daily.** Why: pushing for improvement. Better: clear feedback, not a barrage; otherwise they leave.
- **Taking credit and assigning blame.** Why: ego and pressure. Better: reverse it — credit out, blame in.

## How do I keep a large team aligned?

### Short Answer
Make sure everyone knows the same small set of company goals — pick 10 random employees and they should name the same top three. Productivity drops roughly as the square of headcount unless you actively fight it with alignment: a clear roadmap, repeated goals, and a steady operating rhythm. Repeat the goals far more often than feels necessary, and write down how and why you do things.

### Key Principles
- Alignment beats democracy: if everyone knows and agrees on the top three goals, they self-organize; if not, the company stalls.
- Productivity declines as the square of headcount (more people = more communication overhead) unless you counter it deliberately.
- Repeat the goals obsessively — far more often than you think you need to — on walls, in one-on-ones, at all-hands.
- Establish an operating rhythm: weekly management meetings, monthly all-hands, quarterly planning, occasional offsites.
- Write down how (process) and why (values) so alignment survives rapid hiring.

### Framework
- Test alignment → ask 10 random employees the top three goals; if answers diverge, you have an alignment problem.
- Headcount is rising → invest in roadmap clarity and repeated messaging to counter the square-law drag.
- Setting cadence → weekly leadership sync, monthly all-hands, quarterly planning, periodic offsites.
- New people joining fast → give them the written how-and-why.

### Checklist
- [ ] Everyone can name the same top 3 company goals
- [ ] You repeat goals far more often than feels necessary
- [ ] You have a weekly/monthly/quarterly operating rhythm
- [ ] You've written down how and why you do things
- [ ] You're actively fighting communication overhead as you grow

### Common Mistakes
- **Assuming people know the goals.** Why: you said them once. Better: test it; repeat them constantly.
- **Ignoring communication overhead at scale.** Why: it creeps up invisibly. Better: counter it with alignment and rhythm.
- **No operating rhythm.** Why: meetings feel like overhead. Better: cadence keeps a growing company synced.

## How do I make tough people decisions fairly (raises, demotions, equity)?

### Short Answer
See every decision through the eyes of everyone affected — not just yourself and not just the person in the room, but the people who aren't there and will hear about it. A raise given for asking teaches everyone to lobby; a demotion that leaves someone's title-level equity intact tells the team failure has no cost. Use formal processes for compensation and promotion; they protect the culture better than ad hoc deals.

### Key Principles
- The core management skill is seeing a decision through all stakeholders' eyes, especially those not in the room.
- Every comp/equity/promotion decision is a signal about what the company values (performance vs. negotiation, loyalty vs. merit).
- Use formal, scheduled processes for raises and promotions; informality rewards the best negotiators, not the best performers.
- Don't bury problems to avoid awkwardness — "the deeper you bury it, the hotter it gets."
- When pressured and unsure, pause: "I'm taking this seriously and need to think it through" beats a fast, destructive call.

### Framework
- Someone asks for a raise → run it through a formal process, not a private deal, or you teach everyone to lobby.
- Considering a demotion → ask what it signals about the cost of failure and whether the person can keep credibility (and whether their equity still fits).
- Designing any policy → ask what it signals, how each stakeholder reads it, and what behavior it encourages.
- Big decision under pressure → pause and think through all perspectives before acting.

### Checklist
- [ ] You've considered every affected stakeholder, including those not present
- [ ] You've identified what the decision signals about your values
- [ ] Compensation and promotion run through a formal process
- [ ] You're not quietly burying a problem
- [ ] You paused on high-stakes calls instead of reacting

### Common Mistakes
- **Giving a raise quietly to whoever asks.** Why: it's easy and keeps one person happy. Better: a formal process; otherwise you reward asking over performing.
- **Demoting without addressing equity/credibility.** Why: it feels kinder than firing. Better: think through what the team infers; sometimes a clean exit is better.
- **Burying a problem.** Why: avoiding conflict. Better: address it; it only gets hotter.

## How does my job change as the company grows?

### Short Answer
The biggest shift is around 25 employees, when your job moves from building a great product to building a great company: structure, hiring, delegation, and culture. Put in simple, clear management structure (every person has one manager) — but don't innovate on org design; keep it boring and standard. Innovate on your product and business model, not your management.

### Key Principles
- Around 25 people, the founder's job shifts from "build a great product" to "build a great company."
- Flat structure works under ~20 people but breaks suddenly around 30; you need clear reporting (every person has one manager, every manager knows their reports).
- Don't innovate on management structure — use boring, standard practices; innovate on product and business model instead.
- Get out of "hero mode": accept short-term pain (e.g., falling behind on tickets) to hire and build for scale.
- Build good HR (clear feedback, comp bands, career paths) and an organization system to track your own goals and every person's work.

### Framework
- Under ~20 people → flat is fine.
- Approaching ~25–30 people → install simple, clear reporting lines and a basic management structure.
- Tempted to design a novel org structure → don't; keep it standard and spend your creativity on product.
- Drowning in work → exit hero mode by hiring, even if metrics dip short-term.

### Checklist
- [ ] You recognize the ~25-person product-to-company shift
- [ ] Every person has exactly one manager
- [ ] Your structure is simple and standard, not novel
- [ ] You've exited hero mode by hiring
- [ ] You have good HR basics and a personal organization system

### Common Mistakes
- **Keeping flat structure too long.** Why: it feels cool and lean. Better: it breaks at ~30; install clear reporting first.
- **Over-engineering org structure.** Why: founders love novel systems. Better: keep management boring; innovate on product.
- **Staying in hero mode.** Why: leading by example. Better: hire to scale, even at short-term cost.

# Operations, Legal & Equity

## What legal and incorporation basics must I get right?

### Short Answer
Keep it simple and standard: incorporate in Delaware using a cheap online service (~$200–300), use standard documents, sign everything, and store the originals safely. Founders don't need to master the mechanics — getting bogged down in legal detail is itself dangerous — but a few basics, skipped, can cost you a fortune (one botched out-of-state entity took four law firms and ~$500K to unwind).

### Key Principles
- Incorporate in Delaware with a standard service; investors expect it and the law is settled.
- Use standard, open-sourced documents — they mean lower legal fees, faster closes, and investor comfort.
- Actually sign the paperwork: stock purchase agreements, IP assignments, and the 83(b) election aren't optional, and "we talked about equity" is not enough.
- Store all formation docs, agreements, board minutes, and IP assignments safely and organized — they matter most under the stress of due diligence or acquisition.
- Founders shouldn't get lost in legal mechanics; keep it simple and familiar, and don't improvise exotic structures.

### Framework
- Incorporating → Delaware C-corp via a standard online service; don't form a random-state LLC.
- Choosing documents → use standard/open-sourced ones; avoid custom terms.
- After agreeing on equity → sign the stock purchase agreement, IP assignment, and file the 83(b).
- Storing documents → keep organized originals; you'll need them at Series A and acquisition.

### Checklist
- [ ] Incorporated as a Delaware C-corp via a standard service
- [ ] Using standard, open-sourced documents
- [ ] All key documents are signed (not just discussed)
- [ ] IP assignments collected from founders and employees
- [ ] Documents stored safely and organized

### Common Mistakes
- **Forming an LLC in a random state.** Why: convenience or local advice. Better: Delaware C-corp; conversions are brutally expensive.
- **Not signing documents.** Why: assuming a verbal agreement suffices. Better: sign everything; talk is not a transaction.
- **Storing documents haphazardly.** Why: they seem unimportant early. Better: organize them; due diligence is painful without them.

## How should vesting and the 83(b) election work?

### Short Answer
Everyone — including solo founders — should vest, on the Silicon Valley standard of four years with a one-year cliff. When you receive restricted stock, you must file an 83(b) election with the IRS (and keep proof you filed) — there's no way to fix it later, and deals have died over it. Vesting protects both the company and the founders by ensuring an early departure doesn't leave dead equity behind.

### Key Principles
- Standard vesting is four years with a one-year cliff: leave before a year and you get nothing; after a year, 25%, then monthly.
- Even solo founders should vest — it signals skin in the game and lets you credibly require employees to vest.
- File the 83(b) election when you get restricted stock; keep proof — it cannot be fixed retroactively.
- If a founder leaves early, the company repurchases the unvested shares at the original price, keeping the cap table clean.
- "Stock doesn't buy itself" — complete the paperwork (purchase agreement + 83(b)) or it isn't real.

### Framework
- Setting up founder equity → 4-year vest, 1-year cliff, for everyone including solo founders.
- Receiving restricted stock → file the 83(b) within the deadline and save proof.
- A founder departs before fully vesting → repurchase the unvested shares at original cost.

### Checklist
- [ ] All founders (including solo) are on a 4-year vest with a 1-year cliff
- [ ] 83(b) elections are filed with proof retained
- [ ] Stock purchase agreements are signed
- [ ] Repurchase terms for unvested shares are in place

### Common Mistakes
- **Skipping vesting (especially solo founders).** Why: it feels unnecessary alone. Better: it signals commitment and enables requiring it of employees.
- **Not filing the 83(b) (or losing proof).** Why: it's an obscure form. Better: file on time and keep proof; it can't be fixed later.

## How do I avoid common legal and financial founder mistakes?

### Short Answer
Pay yourself and your cofounders (founders working for free is illegal and creates leverage in breakups), pay your payroll taxes, keep personal and company money strictly separate, and use a real payroll service instead of saving a few dollars a month. These unglamorous basics quietly kill companies: skipped payroll taxes become felony-level problems, and unpaid founder wages become weapons in a cofounder split.

### Key Principles
- Pay founders at least minimum wage; working for free is against the law and becomes leverage for a fired founder claiming wage theft.
- Pay payroll taxes — skipping them is felony-serious, and one company's three-year lapse became a huge, expensive disaster.
- Treat cofounder wages like a marital prenup: paying them removes a major weapon in any future breakup.
- Keep personal and company finances strictly separate — test every expense with "would I be embarrassed to justify this line item to an investor?"
- Use a proper payroll service and option-management software; saving $50–100/month invites $50K+ fines, and spreadsheet equity errors compound into millions.

### Framework
- Setting founder pay → at least local minimum wage; never zero.
- Running payroll → use a service and pay all payroll taxes; don't DIY to save money.
- Spending company money → if you'd be embarrassed to justify it line-by-line to an investor, it's not a business expense.
- Managing options → use real software, not Excel.

### Checklist
- [ ] Founders are paid at least minimum wage
- [ ] Payroll taxes are paid via a real payroll service
- [ ] Personal and company finances are fully separate
- [ ] Every expense passes the "embarrassed to justify it?" test
- [ ] Options are managed in proper software, not a spreadsheet

### Common Mistakes
- **Founders working for free.** Why: saving cash. Better: it's illegal and becomes breakup leverage; pay minimum wage.
- **Skipping payroll taxes.** Why: saving money/time. Better: it's felony-serious; use a service.
- **Mixing personal and company funds.** Why: convenience. Better: separate them; spending investor money on yourself is stealing.

## How should I think about long-term equity?

### Short Answer
Vesting four years upfront isn't enough — give refresher grants year over year so your best early people stay incentivized for the long haul. Establish compensation bands by the time you hit ~25–30 people to prevent chaos when salaries leak. Investors will fight you on the dilution from generous equity; the data favors companies that grant aggressively, so ignore them on this.

### Key Principles
- Refresher grants matter more than the initial grant; top companies grant ~3–5% per year for a decade.
- At year three of a four-year vest, your best people lose their incentive to stay — refresh before then.
- Establish compensation bands (clear ranges per role and level) by ~25–30 people to prevent resentment when pay leaks.
- Investors will resist the dilution from generous equity; ignore them — aggressive granting correlates with outperformance.
- Tie equity and comp to clear career progression so it reads as fair.

### Framework
- Employee approaching year three of vesting → grant refresher equity now.
- Reaching ~25–30 people → set compensation bands tied to role levels.
- Investors push back on dilution from equity grants → grant anyway; the data favors it.

### Checklist
- [ ] You give refresher grants year over year
- [ ] Compensation bands exist by ~25–30 people
- [ ] Equity is tied to clear career progression
- [ ] You're not letting investors talk you out of generous grants

### Common Mistakes
- **Granting only the initial four-year package.** Why: assuming it's done. Better: refresh before year three or lose your best people.
- **No compensation bands.** Why: it feels premature. Better: set them before salaries leak and cause resentment.
- **Caving to investors on dilution.** Why: they push hard. Better: aggressive granting correlates with success.

# Survival, Focus & Psychology

## Why do startups fail?

### Short Answer
Most often because the founders give up, or because they lose focus and momentum — not because the market formally rejects them. The deeper causes are usually building something nobody wants, a bad early hire or cofounder split, running out of money, or simply working hard on the wrong things. You get no points for effort; only for making something the market wants.

### Key Principles
- Most startups die because the founder quits or loses focus, not because the market said a hard no.
- The most fundamental failure is building something nobody wants — going through the motions of a startup without making a product people need.
- A bad early hire or a messy cofounder breakup can be fatal on their own.
- Losing momentum spirals: a team that hasn't won in a while gets demoralized and keeps losing.
- Effort on the wrong things counts for nothing; focus is what converts work into outcomes.

### Framework
- Tempted to quit → recognize this is the most common cause of death; manage your psychology and push through if the thesis holds.
- Momentum is slipping → restore it with small wins, not speeches.
- Working hard but not winning → check you're working on the right things, not just hard.
- A core relationship (cofounder/early hire) is broken → address it fast; these are fatal if left to fester.

### Checklist
- [ ] You're building something people actually want (proven by retention)
- [ ] You're protecting momentum and morale
- [ ] You're working on the right things, not just hard
- [ ] Cofounder and early-hire issues are addressed, not buried
- [ ] You have the runway and resolve to not give up prematurely

### Common Mistakes
- **Giving up too early.** Why: the lows feel unbearable. Better: most deaths are self-inflicted; manage psychology and endure.
- **Going through the motions without making something wanted.** Why: imitating the form of a startup. Better: obsess over a product people need.
- **Letting momentum and morale erode.** Why: distraction and fatigue. Better: manufacture small wins to restore the cycle.

## How do founders avoid losing focus?

### Short Answer
Treat focus as the thing that made you successful and guard it accordingly — most post-success failures come from losing it. Pick the two or three things that matter and say no to everything else, including the seductive distractions of conferences, advising, and side projects. Set clear company goals, repeat them constantly, and don't get pulled off by competitors' press.

### Key Principles
- Focus is what made you successful; losing it is the most common way successful founders fail.
- After some success, burnout tempts you toward easier, more gratifying things (conferences, giving advice, side projects) — resist them.
- Identify the few things that matter and say no to the rest, relentlessly.
- Don't let competitors' press pull your attention; it crushes momentum more than almost anything.
- Keep clear company goals everyone knows, and repeat them far more often than feels necessary.

### Framework
- A shiny opportunity appears (conference, side project) → default to no; it's a focus tax.
- Feeling burnt out → notice the pull toward easy/gratifying work and steer back to what matters.
- Competitor makes noise → ignore it unless they've shipped something that beats you.
- Setting direction → keep two or three goals everyone knows and you repeat constantly.

### Checklist
- [ ] You've named the 2–3 things that matter most
- [ ] You say no to distractions, including prestige ones
- [ ] You're not chasing competitor press
- [ ] Company goals are clear and constantly repeated
- [ ] You guard focus as your core advantage

### Common Mistakes
- **Drifting into the conference/advising circuit after success.** Why: it's gratifying and flattering. Better: focus is what won; protect it.
- **Reacting to competitor PR.** Why: fear. Better: ignore noise; focus on your product.
- **Letting goals go fuzzy.** Why: assuming everyone remembers. Better: repeat them relentlessly.

## How should founders handle uncertainty and their own psychology?

### Short Answer
Accept that managing your own psychology is one of the CEO's core jobs — the highs get higher and the lows get lower as you scale, and the difficulty never goes away (it just changes shape). Expect a long, brutal "trough of sorrow" before things work (Airbnb's took about 1,000 days). Hold conviction in your vision while staying paranoid about execution, and build a support network so you don't quit in a low.

### Key Principles
- Managing your own psychology is a core part of the job, not a weakness — Ben Horowitz calls it the CEO's #1 role.
- It never gets easier; the problems change but the total volume of worry doesn't decrease.
- Your psychology gets more extreme with scale (higher highs, lower lows), and you'll go from rooted-for underdog to publicly criticized — make peace with it early.
- The path runs through a long trough of sorrow with false hope before real growth; prepare for a marathon, not a sprint.
- Hold both conviction (belief in the vision) and paranoia (about execution and criticism) at once; neither cancels the other.

### Framework
- In a deep low → remember most failures are founders quitting in a low; lean on your support network and don't decide to quit there.
- Things feel impossibly hard → normal; difficulty doesn't mean the thesis is wrong (separate the two).
- Riding a high → stay paranoid about execution; highs are as distorting as lows.
- Bracing for the journey → expect ~years in the trough; pace yourself and take vacations to avoid burnout.

### Checklist
- [ ] You treat managing your psychology as core work
- [ ] You expect a long trough before things work
- [ ] You have a support network for the lows
- [ ] You hold conviction and paranoia together
- [ ] You take vacations and pace for a marathon

### Common Mistakes
- **Expecting it to get easier.** Why: success seems like relief. Better: it never gets easier; the problems just change.
- **Deciding to quit during a low.** Why: the lows feel permanent. Better: don't make permanent decisions in a temporary low; use your support network.
- **Ignoring burnout.** Why: hustle culture. Better: pace yourself; it's a marathon.

## How do I keep momentum?

### Short Answer
Treat momentum and growth as the lifeblood of the startup — if you ever take your foot off the gas, things spiral. A winning team stays motivated and keeps winning; a team that hasn't won in a while gets demoralized and keeps losing. When momentum slips, restore it with a series of small wins, not speeches, and establish a steady operating rhythm so progress is constant and visible.

### Key Principles
- Momentum and growth are the lifeblood; take your foot off the gas and things spiral out of control.
- Winning and losing are self-reinforcing states; a winning team keeps winning, a losing team keeps losing.
- Restore lost momentum with small wins, not motivational speeches (Facebook's growth group turned the company around with many tiny improvements).
- "Sales fix everything" — concrete wins re-energize a team faster than anything else.
- Set an operating rhythm: ship and launch regularly, review metrics weekly, so progress is constant and felt.

### Framework
- Momentum is slipping → engineer a string of small, concrete wins to restart the cycle.
- Team morale is sagging → deliver results, not speeches.
- Building durability → establish a rhythm of regular shipping and weekly metric reviews.
- Things are going well → keep the foot on the gas; don't get distracted.

### Checklist
- [ ] You treat growth/momentum as the top priority
- [ ] You restore slumps with small wins, not speeches
- [ ] You ship and launch on a regular cadence
- [ ] You review metrics weekly
- [ ] You don't take your foot off the gas when things go well

### Common Mistakes
- **Taking the foot off the gas after a win.** Why: relief and distraction. Better: momentum spirals down fast; keep pushing.
- **Trying to fix a slump with speeches.** Why: rallying feels like leadership. Better: deliver small concrete wins.

## Should I consider an acquisition offer?

### Short Answer
Don't even start acquisition conversations unless you'd accept a "low" offer — they distract the team, drag out, and demoralize everyone if the deal dies, costing you months and your focus. If there's going to be a big offer, the acquirer will simply send it; you don't need to go fishing for it. Treat acquisition talks as a serious threat to momentum, not a fun option to explore.

### Key Principles
- Acquisition conversations distract and can kill companies; they consume months of founder time and team focus.
- Don't initiate talks unless you'd genuinely accept a low offer — if a big offer is coming, they'll send it without you fishing.
- A dragged-out negotiation that dies is demoralizing and breaks momentum.
- Don't hope for a miracle outcome from "just seeing what they offer."
- Your default posture toward unsolicited interest should protect focus, not chase optionality.

### Framework
- Considering opening acquisition talks → only if you'd accept a low offer; otherwise don't start.
- Expecting a big offer → wait; serious acquirers send strong offers unprompted.
- An acquirer is sniffing around → guard your time and the team's focus; don't let it derail the company.

### Checklist
- [ ] You'd accept a "low" offer before starting any talks
- [ ] You're not fishing for an offer that would arrive anyway
- [ ] You're protecting team focus and momentum
- [ ] You're not banking on a miracle outcome

### Common Mistakes
- **"Let's just see what they offer."** Why: curiosity and ego. Better: it costs months and morale; only engage if you'd accept a low offer.
- **Fishing for a big offer.** Why: hoping to trigger one. Better: real acquirers send strong offers unprompted.

## How long does this really take, and should I commit?

### Short Answer
Assume it takes over your life for years — likely a decade or more — and that it never gets easier; if that terrifies you, don't do it. The best reason to start is that you can't not do it: you're passionate enough to push through the hard years, the world needs it, and you're the right person to build it. Don't romanticize the glamour; the reality is hard work, stress, and a very long road.

### Key Principles
- A startup takes over your life for years — several at minimum, possibly the rest of your working life — and it never gets easier.
- The best reason to start: you "can't not do it" — passion strong enough to carry you through, plus the world needing it and you being the right person.
- Don't be seduced by the romanticized narrative; the reality is customer support emails, sales, and stress, not the movie version.
- You're committing for roughly 5–10 years; leaving early is costly to your career and the people who depend on you.
- Waiting until you're genuinely ready (and have a real idea you can't stop thinking about) doesn't hurt your odds — it usually helps.

### Framework
- Deciding whether to start → if you can't not do it (passion + the world needs it + you're the right person), go; if you're merely intrigued, wait.
- Terrified of starting → probably don't, unless you're energized by doing things you fear.
- Merely unsure you can do it → the only way to find out is to try — just not necessarily right now.
- Expecting it to be glamorous → recalibrate; commit for the long, hard road.

### Checklist
- [ ] You genuinely can't not do this idea
- [ ] You believe the world needs it and you're the right person
- [ ] You've accepted a 5–10+ year commitment that never gets easier
- [ ] You're not motivated mainly by the romanticized image
- [ ] You're starting because you're ready, not because of pressure to start now

### Common Mistakes
- **Starting for the glamour or status.** Why: the narrative is seductive. Better: start only if you can't not do it; passion fuels the hard years.
- **Underestimating the time and toll.** Why: outsiders don't see the difficulty. Better: assume a decade-long, never-easier commitment.
- **Rushing to start before you're ready.** Why: fear of missing the window. Better: waiting often improves your odds.
